# Writing rules: reference

Sources and reasoning behind the rules in SKILL.md. Written for scientific papers; the same reasoning
carries over to essays, with the venue and the reader swapped. Section numbers here are this file's own.

The failure this prevents: prose that is fluent, organized, factually correct, and immediately
recognizable as machine-written. Fluency is not the target. The target is text a referee in the
field reads without noticing the writing at all.

Work top to bottom. Most register failures are decided before the first sentence exists, and no
sentence-level edit repairs them.

## 1. Before writing: readers, value, tension

Larry McEnerney (University of Chicago Writing Program, *The Craft of Writing Effectively*) states
the mistake plainly: "You think that writing is communicating your ideas to your readers. It is
not." And: "No one cares about the inside of your head."

Three consequences.

- **Value is assigned by readers, not by the work.** "There's no such thing as value here. Value is
  here." Nothing is valuable because it was hard, because it is true, or because it is yours. It is
  valuable because a specific community can use it. Name that community before writing.
- **Readers are searching for instability.** "Your readers are actually searching for a language of
  instability, inconsistency, and tension." A draft that says "here is a thing we built and it
  works" gives the reader nothing to do. A draft that says "the field believes X, and under
  condition Y that breaks" has a reader.
- **Codes are local.** "You must know the codes of the communities you're working in." The codes are
  discoverable: read ten recent papers from the target venue and copy their conventions.

## 2. Outline before prose

Whitesides (*Adv. Mater.* 16:1375, 2004): "think of an outline as a carefully organized and
presented set of data, with attendant objectives, hypotheses, and conclusions, rather than an
outline of text", organized "in order of importance, not in chronological order".

So the outline is the figures, tables and equations in order of importance, each with the one
sentence it licenses. Patching sentences in text that was never planned is the most common cause of
register slide, and it is invisible from inside the sentence being patched.

## 3. Structure: the story shape

Joshua Schimel (*Writing Science*, OUP 2012) gives the shape as **OCAR**: Opening, Challenge,
Action, Resolution. "A story lacking any element from it will be unsatisfying, ineffective, and
slippery, rather than sticky."

- **Opening** is a power position. It sets the scale of the whole paper, so it states the broad
  question, not the method. Opening on a technical detail and then widening produces what Schimel
  calls mental whiplash.
- **Challenge** is the knowledge gap. Framing it is the highest-leverage move in the introduction:
  "It's like framing a window." The gap defines what counts as a result. If the reader cannot state
  the gap after the introduction, the paper has no result.
- The **funnel** connects opening to challenge, and every paragraph between them narrows.
- **Resolution** answers the challenge as posed, in the same words it was posed in.

At paragraph scale, **context, content, conclusion** (Mensh & Kording, *PLoS Comput Biol* 2017):
first sentence sets the topic, the body carries what is new, the last states what is to be
remembered. The same shape governs the abstract and the paper.

**One message.** Mensh & Kording: "Papers that simultaneously focus on multiple contributions tend
to be less convincing about each." Several contributions crowded into one abstract is the clearest
symptom that the paper has not decided what it is.

**No zig-zag.** Each subject appears in exactly one place, only the central idea recurs, and
parallel messages take parallel grammatical form.

**Pick the niche deliberately** (Swales, CARS): counter a claim, indicate a gap, raise a question, or
continue a tradition. Writing "indicate a gap" when the honest move is "continue a tradition" forces
the prose into advocacy, and advocacy reads as marketing.

## 4. The abstract, and how to calibrate it to a venue

The general template, from Nature's own summary-paragraph guidance: one or two sentences of
field-level introduction, two or three of background, **one** stating the problem, **one** giving
the result ("here we show"), two or three on what it changes relative to prior belief, one or two of
general context. 190 to 250 words, 300 absolute maximum, and it "avoids numbers ... unless
essential".

Do not stop at the template. Venues and subfields differ, and the difference is measurable in about
twenty minutes. **The calibration procedure**, which is worth running once per target venue:

1. Collect six to ten recent abstracts: the target journal or conference, plus the group whose
   lineage the work sits in.
2. Count, per abstract: total words, numeric results quoted, claim verbs used, whether the first
   sentence contains a method.
3. Write the medians down in the piece's settings (SKILL.md section 7). Those medians are the spec, and the template above
   is only the prior.

Fetch and count directly; do not estimate. When this was run on seven preprints from one theory group,
a second-hand count was wrong by up to eighteen words per abstract and asserted a claim-verb pattern
that one of the seven violated.

Which way to lean before you have measured: an abstract that headlines a capacity or a mechanism is
safe almost everywhere; one that headlines a benchmark score is a register mismatch in most theory
venues, however good the number.

## 5. Sentences: five tests, run on each one

Gopen & Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing" (*American Scientist* 78:550, 1990). These are
principles, not rules. A deliberate violation at a moment that deserves weight is craft. An
accidental one every third sentence is the defect.

1. **Subject next to verb.** Anything of length between them is read as an interruption. The common
   failure: subject, then a relative clause carrying the real content, then finally the verb.
2. **Stress position.** The end of the sentence carries the emphasis, so the new information goes
   there and nothing else does. Their test for "too long": more candidate stress positions (colons,
   semicolons, clause breaks) than the sentence has stress positions.
3. **Topic position.** "Readers expect a unit of discourse to be a story about whoever shows up
   first." If the first thing is not what the sentence is about, rewrite it.
4. **Old before new.** Open on what the reader already has, close on what is new. They call the
   violation the number one problem in professional writing, and it is what makes a paragraph read
   as a list of true statements rather than as an argument.
5. **Action in the verb.** No nominalization hung off "provides", "exhibits", "is characterized by".
   "A need exists for greater selection efficiency" is "we must select more efficiently".

## 6. Named defects, delete on sight

Steven Pinker, "Why Academic Writing Stinks" (*Chronicle of Higher Education*, 26 Sept 2014).

- **Classic style is the goal.** "The writer knows the truth before putting it into words; he is not
  using the occasion of writing to sort out what he thinks." Prose that narrates its own reasoning
  is a draft, not a paper.
- **The curse of knowledge.** The default failure is not vanity, it is being unable to imagine not
  already knowing the thing. Every unexplained term is an instance.
- **Metadiscourse.** Signposting about the text itself ("in this section we will first discuss") "is
  there to help the writer, not the reader". Cut it, or replace it with the question the section
  answers.
- **Professional narcissism.** Writing about the state of the field's research activity instead of
  about the world the research is about.
- **Apologizing.** Prefacing a claim with how difficult and contested the topic is.
- **Hedging.** "Hedging is a choice, not a tic." One hedge, at the honest strength, never a bare
  claim followed by a clause taking it back.
- **Metaconcepts and zombie nouns.** "level", "issue", "framework", "approach", "perspective" where
  the concrete thing belongs, and nominalizations that turn a verb into a noun and then need a fuzzy
  verb to carry it.

*Nature Immunology* editorial (2005) on persuasion adverbs: "remarkably", "interestingly",
"surprisingly" and kin "persuade, something that has no place in scientific manuscripts". If the
data are remarkable the reader needs no prompting.

## 7. Words and constructions (notes)

Two tiers. The first is short on purpose, because a long prohibition list is skimmed and then
ignored. The second is a rule for generating the rest.

**Em dashes.** Whether these are banned is a per-piece decision, and where a piece bans them the ban
is absolute: not the character, not an en dash used as one, not LaTeX `---`, and not `--` between
clauses, though `--` inside a numeric range is fine. The replacement is a full stop (usually right),
a comma, a colon when a definition or list follows, or parentheses. If none of those fit, the
sentence carries two thoughts and becomes two sentences. Independent of any ban, that last test is
worth running: an em dash often marks a sentence that was never resolved.

## 8. Terminology discipline

The principle is general even though every instance is local.

- **Do not coin.** A term for an object must already exist in the comparable literature with the
  same meaning. A quantity that is genuinely new gets a symbol and a one-sentence definition in
  Methods, not a name.
- **Do not borrow a term that carries someone else's derivation.** Established terms come with
  commitments: a mechanism, a derivation, sometimes a specific model. Using one for a thing that
  does not satisfy those commitments will be read as either a claim you did not make or an error you
  did not catch. When the nearest established term almost fits, describe the object instead.
- **Match the field's word, not the more precise word.** Where the literature has settled on a
  phrase, use it even if it is clumsier. Counting occurrences across ten recent papers settles this
  faster than arguing it.
- **One name per object, everywhere,** including figure labels, captions, and axis titles.

Each piece's actual banned and required terms go in its settings, so they can be searched for
mechanically rather than remembered.

## 9. Revision: three passes, largest first

Never revise for sentences before the structure is settled, because most of those sentences will be
deleted.

1. **Structure.** Does each section answer the challenge it was set? Is there exactly one message?
   Does the resolution use the words the challenge used? Fixes here are moves and deletions.
2. **Paragraph.** Does every paragraph have context, content, conclusion? Does the last sentence
   state what is to be remembered? Is old information leading each paragraph?
3. **Sentence.** The five tests, section 5, one sentence at a time. Then the word list, section 7.

Between passes 1 and 2, read the paper by its figures alone: captions plus the first and last
sentence of each section. If the argument does not survive that reading, the problem is structural
and no sentence pass will find it.

Then run the skim test as its own read, because it fails differently: pull the section titles and
the first sentence of each section into a list and read only that. A section whose first sentence
sets up rather than states is the defect, and it is invisible while reading the section in full.
